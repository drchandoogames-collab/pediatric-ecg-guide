# Pediatric ECG Teaching Manual for Residents

A concise, case-oriented guide to pediatric ECG interpretation for postgraduate trainees.  
Target audience: Pediatrics residents, emergency residents, and junior fellows.[web:7][web:37][web:52]

---

## 1. Why Pediatric ECGs Are Different

- Age-dependent norms for heart rate, axis, intervals, and voltages.
- Physiological right ventricular dominance in neonates and infants, with gradual shift to left ventricular dominance through childhood.
- Benign age-related patterns (e.g., sinus arrhythmia, juvenile T-wave pattern) that can be mistaken for pathology.[web:7][web:14][web:52]

**Key points for residents**

- Always interpret pediatric ECGs in the context of age, clinical presentation, and hemodynamic status.[web:7][web:37][web:52]  
- Avoid applying adult thresholds to children; what is normal at 2 years may be abnormal in an adolescent.[web:7][web:42][web:52]

## 2. Standard ECG Setup and Quality Checks

Before interpretation, confirm:

- Correct **patient**: name, ID, age, and date/time on the tracing.
- Standard **paper speed**: 25 mm/s (50 mm/s may be used for tachyarrhythmias).
- Standard **amplitude**: 10 mm/mV.
- Proper **lead placement**; misplacement can mimic ischemia, axis deviation, or chamber hypertrophy.[web:41][web:47][web:52]

Practical checklist:

1. Confirm patient identity and age on the ECG.
2. Verify paper speed and gain (25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV).
3. Look for artefacts (muscle tremor, loose cables, electrical interference).
4. Re-check lead placement if there are global or bizarre abnormalities.[web:41][web:47]

## 3. Stepwise Approach to Pediatric ECG Interpretation

Use the same sequence every time.[web:7][web:37][web:52]

### 3.1 Rate

- At 25 mm/s, 1 large box = 0.20 s; rate ≈ 300 / number of large boxes between R–R for regular rhythms.
- Compare to age-specific normal ranges.[web:7][web:37][web:42]

Approximate resting heart rates:

- Neonate: 100–160 bpm  
- Infant: 100–150 bpm  
- 1–5 years: 80–130 bpm  
- 6–12 years: 70–110 bpm  
- Adolescent: 60–100 bpm[web:7][web:42][web:52]

### 3.2 Rhythm

- **Normal sinus rhythm**  
  - P wave before every QRS, constant PR, P positive in leads I and II.
- **Sinus arrhythmia**  
  - Mildly irregular R–R, often respiratory; normal in children.[web:7][web:14][web:52]

Red flags:

- No visible P waves, or P waves not related to QRS (AV dissociation).
- Irregularly irregular rhythm not explained by sinus arrhythmia.
- Very rapid narrow-complex tachycardia with absent/retrograde P waves → think SVT.[web:7][web:37]

### 3.3 Axis

- Estimate QRS axis using leads I and aVF (or I and III).
- Typical pediatric pattern:
  - Newborn: rightward axis (up to about +160°).
  - Child/adolescent: shifts toward 0° to +90°.[web:7][web:37][web:52]

Concerning findings:

- Left axis deviation in older children → consider AV canal defect or left anterior fascicular block.
- Marked right axis deviation beyond age expectations → consider right ventricular hypertrophy or pulmonary hypertension.[web:7][web:37]

### 3.4 P Waves (Atria)

Assess P waves in II and V1:

- Right atrial enlargement: tall peaked P in II (>3 mm), large initial positive component in V1.
- Left atrial enlargement: broad/notched P in II, deep terminal negative component in V1.[web:7][web:14][web:49]

### 3.5 QRS Complex (Ventricles)

Look at:

- QRS duration: should be narrow; prolonged QRS suggests bundle branch block or ventricular rhythm.
- R/S progression in chest leads.
- Voltages relative to age:
  - Early life: physiologic right ventricular dominance (R in V1, S in V6).
  - Later childhood: left ventricular dominance (tall R in V5–V6).[web:7][web:14][web:52]

Patterns:

- Right ventricular hypertrophy: dominant R in V1, right axis, possible strain changes.
- Left ventricular hypertrophy: tall R in left precordials, deep S in right precordials, with or without ST–T changes.[web:14][web:37]

### 3.6 ST Segments and T Waves

- Juvenile T-wave pattern: T inversion in V1–V3 is often normal in children and adolescents.
- Diffuse, concave ST elevation with PR depression → pericarditis.
- Focal ST elevation with reciprocal changes → ischemia/infarction (e.g., Kawasaki disease, anomalous coronaries, myocarditis).[web:7][web:37][web:52]

### 3.7 Intervals (PR, QRS, QT)

- PR interval: increases with age and slower rates; prolonged PR suggests first-degree AV block.
- QRS duration: shorter in children; marked prolongation indicates conduction delay.
- QT interval: measure QT and correct (QTc); prolonged QTc beyond age/sex norms raises concern for long QT syndrome.[web:7][web:42][web:51]

## 4. Age-Related Normal Features

### 4.1 Neonate and Infant

- Higher resting heart rates and rightward axis.
- RV dominance with tall R in V1 and deep S in V6.
- T waves upright in V1 initially, then invert after the first week.[web:7][web:37][web:52]

### 4.2 Child

- Gradual shift to LV dominance and adult-like axis.
- Juvenile T-wave pattern common: T inversion in V1–V3 without other abnormalities.[web:7][web:14][web:52]

### 4.3 Adolescent

- ECG resembles adult pattern.
- Some may retain juvenile T waves in right precordial leads.
- Resting heart rate approaches adult values.[web:7][web:42]

## 5. Benign Variants vs Red Flags

| Pattern                        | Usually benign                                      | Red flag features / action                    |
|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Sinus arrhythmia              | Common, respiration related                        | Associated syncope, pauses, or bradycardia    |
| Juvenile T-wave pattern       | T inversion V1–V3 in well child/adolescent         | Deep/widespread inversion, symptoms, family hx|
| Mild isolated LVH voltage     | Asymptomatic, athletic child                       | LVH + ST–T changes or symptoms                |
| Early repolarization pattern  | Up-sloping ST elevation in athletic adolescent     | ST elevation with reciprocal depression       |
| Mild first-degree AV block    | Asymptomatic, often in athletes                    | Marked PR, bradycardia, or associated symptoms|[web:7][web:14][web:37][web:52]

## 6. High-Yield Pathologies for Residents

Focus on conditions with high clinical impact and misinterpretation risk.[web:6][web:39][web:46]

### 6.1 Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT)

- Rapid, regular narrow-complex tachycardia (often >180–200 bpm in children).
- P waves absent, buried, or retrograde.
- Presents with palpitations, poor feeding, irritability, or heart failure in infants.[web:7][web:37]

Teaching points:

- Differentiate from sinus tachycardia (sudden onset/offset, very fixed rate, absent normal P morphology).
- Link ECG findings with acute management protocols (vagal maneuvers, adenosine) per local guidelines.

### 6.2 Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)

- Wide-complex regular tachycardia, often >150–160 bpm.
- AV dissociation, capture or fusion beats when visible.
- Often associated with structural heart disease, myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, or channelopathy.[web:7][web:37][web:52]

### 6.3 Long QT Syndrome

- QTc repeatedly above normal for age and sex (commonly ≥460–480 ms depending on guideline).
- History of syncope, seizure-like episodes, or family history of sudden death.
- Avoid QT-prolonging drugs; arrange urgent cardiology review.[web:7][web:42][web:51]

### 6.4 Pre-excitation (e.g., WPW)

- Short PR interval, delta wave (slurred upstroke of QRS), widened QRS.
- Risk of SVT and, rarely, sudden death with rapid AF conduction.[web:7][web:37][web:52]

### 6.5 Myocarditis / Pericarditis

- ECG may show diffuse ST changes, low voltages, conduction blocks, or arrhythmias.
- Context: recent viral illness, chest pain, heart failure signs, or shock.
- Requires troponin, echocardiography, and close monitoring.[web:7][web:37]

## 7. Common Pitfalls in Pediatric ECG Reading

- Using adult normal values for rate, axis, intervals, and voltages in all ages.
- Overcalling pathology in juvenile T-wave patterns.
- Missing long QT due to incorrect QT measurement or rate correction.
- Ignoring the clinical scenario and reading the ECG in isolation.
- Misinterpreting artefact or lead misplacement as true disease.[web:6][web:7][web:14][web:49]

Teaching suggestions:

- Use paired examples of normal variants vs genuine pathology.
- Make residents verbalize their stepwise approach whenever presenting an ECG.

## 8. Indications for Obtaining a Pediatric ECG

Common reasons to request an ECG include:

- Syncope, near-syncope, or seizure with concerning features.
- Palpitations or exertional chest pain.
- Murmur or signs suggestive of structural heart disease.
- Family history of cardiomyopathy, channelopathy, or sudden unexplained death.
- Drug exposure with risk of QT prolongation or cardiotoxicity.[web:37][web:43][web:45]

## 9. Teaching and Assessment Plan

Evidence shows that structured teaching and cardiology exposure improve resident ECG skills.[web:6][web:39][web:46]

Suggested framework:

1. **Intro session (60–90 min)**  
   - Basics: setup, age-related norms, systematic approach.
2. **Case-based workshops**  
   - 8–10 ECGs: normal, sinus arrhythmia, juvenile T waves, SVT, VT, long QT, myocarditis, CHD.
3. **Interpretation logbook**  
   - Each resident interprets a set number of ECGs per posting with consultant feedback.
4. **Pre- and post-tests**  
   - Use MCQs or vignette-based ECG questions to document improvement.[web:6][web:46]

## 10. Suggested Structured ECG Report for Residents

Residents can use this format when presenting an ECG:

1. **Demographics**: age, sex, indication for ECG.  
2. **Technical quality**: paper speed, gain, artefact, lead placement issues.  
3. **Rate and rhythm**: heart rate, rhythm, conduction (sinus, SVT, VT, blocks).  
4. **Axis**: QRS axis (normal/right/left) with brief comment.  
5. **Intervals**: PR, QRS, QT/QTc compared with age norms.  
6. **Waves and segments**: P waves, QRS voltages, ST segments, T-wave morphology.  
7. **Overall impression**: normal / normal variant / abnormal, with specific suspected diagnosis.  
8. **Plan**: repeat ECG, further tests, cardiology referral, or acute management as indicated.
